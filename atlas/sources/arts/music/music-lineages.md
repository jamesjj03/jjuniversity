# Music lineages source brief

This seed note grounds an Atlas map about music lineages. It is not a complete music history. It gives the generator and reviewer compact material about traditions, scenes, stylistic inheritance, representative figures, recordings or text anchors, and relation cues.

## European classical and art-music lineages

European classical music develops through liturgical, court, public concert, and conservatory institutions. J. S. Bach anchors contrapuntal craft and Lutheran sacred music. Haydn, Mozart, and Beethoven shape classical form, symphony, chamber music, and public musical authorship. Wagner, Brahms, Debussy, Stravinsky, Schoenberg, and later composers push harmony, orchestration, tonality, rhythm, and modernism in different directions.

The pressure point is canon. A good map should show the lineage without treating one institutional tradition as the default measure of all music.

## African diasporic blues, gospel, and jazz

This lineage grows through spirituals, work songs, blues, gospel, ragtime, New Orleans ensemble practice, swing, bebop, modal jazz, free jazz, and fusion. Bessie Smith, Robert Johnson, Louis Armstrong, Duke Ellington, Charlie Parker, Dizzy Gillespie, Billie Holiday, Miles Davis, John Coltrane, Thelonious Monk, and Ornette Coleman represent different turns in improvisation, harmony, rhythm, voice, and ensemble logic.

The pressure point is reduction. The reviewer should avoid collapsing distinct Black musical traditions into a single influence stream while still showing real inheritance and transformation.

## Folk, country, and singer-songwriter traditions

Folk and country lineages organize around vernacular song, balladry, dance music, oral transmission, radio, recording, and regional identity. The Carter Family and Jimmie Rodgers anchor early commercial country. Woody Guthrie and Pete Seeger shape political folk revival. Hank Williams, Johnny Cash, Bob Dylan, Dolly Parton, Joni Mitchell, and Townes Van Zandt show different bridges between folk, country, poetry, and popular song.

The pressure point is authenticity. A good map should ask how industry, regional identity, race, class, and authorship shape claims about roots and tradition.

## Rock, soul, funk, and amplified popular music

This lineage grows from rhythm and blues, gospel, blues, country, youth culture, amplification, studio recording, and mass media. Chuck Berry, Little Richard, Elvis Presley, The Beatles, The Rolling Stones, Jimi Hendrix, Aretha Franklin, James Brown, Sly Stone, Led Zeppelin, Stevie Wonder, and Prince mark different paths through guitar, voice, rhythm, arrangement, performance, and studio experimentation.

The pressure point is borrowing. The reviewer should make racial, commercial, and technological transfer visible rather than treating genre change as a neutral chain.

## Hip-hop and sample-based lineages

Hip-hop begins as a Bronx block-party culture of DJing, MCing, breakdance, graffiti, sound systems, and community competition. DJ Kool Herc, Grandmaster Flash, Afrika Bambaataa, Run-DMC, Public Enemy, Rakim, N.W.A, A Tribe Called Quest, Tupac Shakur, The Notorious B.I.G., Jay-Z, Missy Elliott, OutKast, Kanye West, Kendrick Lamar, and many others define shifts in flow, production, politics, region, and industry.

The pressure point is scope. A good map should show hip-hop as culture, technique, market, and political voice without reducing it to lyrics alone.

## Electronic, dance, and studio technology lineages

Electronic and dance music lineages grow through tape, synthesizers, drum machines, samplers, sequencers, clubs, DJs, and studio systems. Pierre Schaeffer, Karlheinz Stockhausen, Wendy Carlos, Kraftwerk, Giorgio Moroder, Juan Atkins, Derrick May, Kevin Saunderson, Frankie Knuckles, Larry Levan, Aphex Twin, Bjork, Daft Punk, and many others show experimental, disco, techno, house, ambient, and pop routes.

The pressure point is authorship. The reviewer should ask how machines, scenes, producers, DJs, dancers, and platforms share creative agency.

## Global popular and diasporic exchange

This lineage tracks how musical forms travel through migration, colonization, radio, records, clubs, streaming, and local adaptation. Reggae and dub move through figures like Bob Marley, Lee Scratch Perry, and King Tubby. Afrobeat through Fela Kuti connects highlife, jazz, funk, and politics. Salsa, cumbia, samba, Bollywood film music, K-pop, reggaeton, Afrobeats, and other scenes show how local industries and diasporas reshape global sound.

The pressure point is map scale. A reviewable map should avoid token global coverage while still showing cross-border exchange as central to modern music.

## Experimental, punk, and independent scenes

Experimental and independent lineages push against institutional polish, commercial formulas, and genre boundaries. John Cage, La Monte Young, Brian Eno, The Velvet Underground, Patti Smith, The Ramones, The Sex Pistols, Talking Heads, Sonic Youth, Fugazi, Nirvana, Radiohead, and independent scenes show different versions of minimalism, noise, punk, art-rock, DIY, alternative rock, and studio experimentation.

The pressure point is boundary drawing. The reviewer should ask whether a map separates scenes by sound, politics, institutions, technology, or attitude.
