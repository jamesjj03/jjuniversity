# Music lineages editorial anchors

This review note strengthens the music lineages draft by adding recording/text anchors, category balance, and relation logic. It is a focused editorial source brief, not a complete music history.

## Category balance

The music map should not read as a single Western progression from classical music to modern popular music. It should show several simultaneous lineage types: institutional art music, African diasporic improvisation and groove, vernacular song traditions, amplified popular music, hip-hop and sampling, electronic/studio technology, Caribbean sound-system practice, Latin and Afro-diasporic dance exchange, and experimental or independent scenes.

The original global and diasporic group is too broad for publication. Split it into at least two reviewable groups: Caribbean reggae, dub, and sound-system lineages; and Latin, Afro-diasporic, and global pop exchange. This keeps Bob Marley, Lee Scratch Perry, and King Tubby in a sound-system/dub lineage, while Fela Kuti, salsa, cumbia, reggaeton, Afrobeats, and K-pop style global pop circulation can be treated as exchange and industry patterns rather than one flat category.

## Recording and contributor anchors

European classical and art-music lineages can anchor J. S. Bach with The Well-Tempered Clavier and Brandenburg Concertos, Beethoven with Symphony No. 3 and late string quartets, Debussy with Prelude to the Afternoon of a Faun, Stravinsky with The Rite of Spring, and Schoenberg with Pierrot lunaire. The pressure point is canon: this tradition should not be treated as the universal default.

African diasporic blues, gospel, and jazz can anchor Louis Armstrong with West End Blues and Hot Five recordings, Duke Ellington with Black, Brown and Beige, Charlie Parker with Ko-Ko, Miles Davis with Kind of Blue, John Coltrane with A Love Supreme, and Ornette Coleman with The Shape of Jazz to Come. This lineage should foreground improvisation, swing, blue notes, ensemble practice, harmony, and Black musical institutions.

Folk, country, and singer-songwriter traditions can anchor The Carter Family with Wildwood Flower and Bristol session recordings, Jimmie Rodgers with Blue Yodel recordings, Woody Guthrie with Dust Bowl Ballads, Bob Dylan with The Freewheelin' Bob Dylan and Highway 61 Revisited, Dolly Parton with Coat of Many Colors, and Joni Mitchell with Blue. The pressure point is authenticity and industry mediation.

Rock, soul, funk, and amplified popular music can anchor Chuck Berry with Johnny B. Goode, Aretha Franklin with Respect, James Brown with Papa's Got a Brand New Bag and Funky Drummer, Jimi Hendrix with Are You Experienced, Stevie Wonder with Songs in the Key of Life, and Prince with Purple Rain. The relation to Black musical sources and commercial borrowing must be visible.

Hip-hop and sample-based lineages can anchor DJ Kool Herc with breakbeat DJ practice, Grandmaster Flash with The Adventures of Grandmaster Flash on the Wheels of Steel and The Message, Public Enemy with It Takes a Nation of Millions to Hold Us Back, Rakim with Paid in Full, N.W.A with Straight Outta Compton, Missy Elliott with Supa Dupa Fly, OutKast with Aquemini, and Kendrick Lamar with To Pimp a Butterfly. Hip-hop should be treated as culture, production, region, and industry, not lyrics alone.

Electronic, dance, and studio technology lineages can anchor Pierre Schaeffer with Etude aux chemins de fer, Stockhausen with Gesang der Junglinge, Wendy Carlos with Switched-On Bach, Kraftwerk with Autobahn and Trans-Europe Express, Giorgio Moroder with I Feel Love, Juan Atkins and Cybotron with Clear, Frankie Knuckles with Your Love, Aphex Twin with Selected Ambient Works, and Daft Punk with Homework or Discovery. The pressure point is shared authorship among machines, producers, DJs, dancers, scenes, and platforms.

Caribbean reggae, dub, and sound-system lineages can anchor Bob Marley with Catch a Fire and Exodus, Lee Scratch Perry with Super Ape, King Tubby with King Tubby Meets Rockers Uptown, and the broader sound-system tradition as a production and performance infrastructure. This group should connect to hip-hop and electronic music without reducing Caribbean music to an influence source.

Latin, Afro-diasporic, and global pop exchange can anchor Fela Kuti with Zombie and Afrobeat, Celia Cruz with salsa performance and recordings, Tito Puente with mambo and Latin jazz, cumbia and samba as regional dance traditions, Daddy Yankee with Gasolina for reggaeton's global turn, and contemporary Afrobeats and K-pop as examples of platform-era global circulation. This group needs careful wording because it spans multiple regions and should remain a map gateway, not a final category.

Experimental, punk, and independent scenes can anchor John Cage with 4'33" and Silence, The Velvet Underground with The Velvet Underground and Nico, Patti Smith with Horses, The Ramones with Ramones, Talking Heads with Remain in Light, Sonic Youth with Daydream Nation, Fugazi with Repeater, Nirvana with Nevermind, and Radiohead with OK Computer or Kid A. The pressure point is boundary drawing: sound, politics, institutions, and attitude do not always line up.

## Relation review

Jazz and blues feed rock, soul, funk, hip-hop, and global pop, but the map must avoid making Black music only a source for later commercial forms. The relation notes should mention transformation, institutions, borrowing, and power.

Hip-hop borrows from funk, soul, jazz, reggae sound systems, and electronic production while also creating its own global lineage through sampling, flow, region, and media.

Electronic and dance music borrows studio and club technologies while influencing hip-hop, pop, experimental music, and global dance scenes.

Caribbean sound systems connect reggae and dub to hip-hop and electronic remix culture through bass, versions, toasting, DJ practice, and studio manipulation.

Latin, Afro-diasporic, and global pop exchange should relate as a neighbor to Caribbean, hip-hop, electronic, and rock/pop groups, not as the final end point of all music history.

Experimental and independent scenes should relate to art music, punk, electronic studio practice, and rock without becoming a catch-all for everything weird.

## Release checks

Do not publish placeholder text titles such as Various works unless the group clearly uses them as temporary evidence. Do not publish claims that imply a single linear march from European classical music to global pop. Split overly broad global categories where source coverage supports it.
