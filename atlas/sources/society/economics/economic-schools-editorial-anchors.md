# Economic schools editorial anchors

This review note strengthens the economic schools draft by adding concrete text anchors, contributor placement checks, and relation logic. It is a focused editorial source brief, not a complete bibliography.

## Text and contributor anchors by school

Classical political economy should anchor Adam Smith with The Wealth of Nations, David Ricardo with Principles of Political Economy and Taxation, and Thomas Malthus with An Essay on the Principle of Population. Smith belongs as the synthesis point for markets, division of labor, moral philosophy, and political economy. Ricardo belongs for distribution, rent, and comparative advantage. Malthus belongs for scarcity and population pressure.

Marxian economics should anchor Karl Marx with Capital, Rosa Luxemburg with The Accumulation of Capital, and Rudolf Hilferding with Finance Capital. Marx belongs as the theorist of capitalism, labor, surplus, crisis, and historical materialism. Luxemburg and Hilferding belong as extensions into imperialism, accumulation, monopoly, and finance.

Neoclassical and marginalist economics should anchor William Stanley Jevons with The Theory of Political Economy, Leon Walras with Elements of Pure Economics, Carl Menger with Principles of Economics, Alfred Marshall with Principles of Economics, and Paul Samuelson with Foundations of Economic Analysis. This family should not collapse into Austrian economics even though Menger is shared.

Keynesian economics should anchor John Maynard Keynes with The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money, Joan Robinson with The Economics of Imperfect Competition, Nicholas Kaldor with essays on growth and distribution, James Tobin with portfolio and monetary theory work, and Hyman Minsky with Stabilizing an Unstable Economy. Keynes belongs for aggregate demand, uncertainty, involuntary unemployment, and policy response.

Austrian economics should anchor Carl Menger with Principles of Economics, Ludwig von Mises with Human Action and Economic Calculation in the Socialist Commonwealth, Friedrich Hayek with The Use of Knowledge in Society and The Road to Serfdom, and Israel Kirzner with Competition and Entrepreneurship. The Austrian family should center market process, dispersed knowledge, entrepreneurship, and calculation.

Institutional and evolutionary economics should anchor Thorstein Veblen with The Theory of the Leisure Class, John R. Commons with Institutional Economics, Wesley Mitchell with Business Cycles, Douglass North with Institutions, Institutional Change and Economic Performance, and Nelson and Winter with An Evolutionary Theory of Economic Change. This family is about rules, habits, organizations, path dependence, and technological routines.

Behavioral economics should anchor Herbert Simon with Administrative Behavior and bounded rationality, Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky with judgment, heuristics, and prospect theory, and Richard Thaler with behavioral anomalies and policy design. This family should be placed as a critique and repair of strong rational-choice assumptions, not as a total replacement for all economics.

Complexity, ecological, and pluralist economics should be handled carefully. W. Brian Arthur belongs for increasing returns and complexity economics, Elinor Ostrom for governing commons, Herman Daly for steady-state and ecological economics, and Kate Raworth can be used as a recent public-facing pluralist/ecological synthesis. This group is a bridge family and should not pretend all systems or ecological approaches are one school.

## Relation review

Classical political economy is a predecessor for Marxian economics and neoclassical economics, but the relation differs. Marxian economics answers and critiques classical distribution, labor, and capitalist dynamics. Neoclassical economics reframes value and price around marginal choice and equilibrium.

Keynesian economics answers failures of neoclassical full-employment equilibrium by focusing on aggregate demand, uncertainty, and involuntary unemployment.

Austrian economics borrows marginalist subjective value but opposes central planning and strong macro-management through knowledge and calculation arguments.

Institutional economics reframes both market and policy models by making rules, organizations, habits, and historical path dependence central.

Behavioral economics reframes neoclassical rationality by introducing bounded rationality, heuristics, biases, and observed decision behavior.

Complexity and ecological economics reframe equilibrium-centered economics by emphasizing feedback, adaptation, commons governance, limits, and non-equilibrium systems.
