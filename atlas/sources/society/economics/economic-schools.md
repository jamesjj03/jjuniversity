# Economic schools source brief

This seed note grounds an Atlas map about major schools of economic thought. It is not a final scholarly survey. It gives the generator and reviewer a compact set of families, claims, people, text anchors, objections, and relation cues.

## Classical political economy

Classical political economy studies wealth, production, labor, trade, value, rents, and growth in commercial society. Adam Smith gives a canonical synthesis of markets, specialization, moral sentiment, and political economy. David Ricardo sharpens distribution, comparative advantage, and rent. Thomas Malthus gives population pressure and scarcity a central role.

The pressure point is social structure. A good map should show whether classical economics explains distribution and power or treats them as background conditions of production and exchange.

## Marxian economics

Marxian economics treats capitalism as a historical system organized by class relations, labor, surplus extraction, crisis, and accumulation. Karl Marx anchors the family through value, exploitation, commodity fetishism, and historical materialism. Rosa Luxemburg, Rudolf Hilferding, and later Marxian economists extend the analysis into imperialism, finance, monopoly, and crisis theory.

The pressure point is determinism. The reviewer should ask when the school explains real structural conflict and when it overcommits to a single historical mechanism.

## Neoclassical and marginalist economics

Neoclassical economics explains prices and allocation through marginal choice, utility, scarcity, equilibrium, and optimization. William Stanley Jevons, Leon Walras, Carl Menger, Alfred Marshall, and later Paul Samuelson help define the analytic core. The family makes economics mathematically portable across consumers, firms, markets, and welfare questions.

The pressure point is abstraction. The map should ask whether optimization and equilibrium clarify economic choices or hide institutions, history, and bounded human behavior.

## Keynesian and macroeconomic management

Keynesian economics treats aggregate demand, uncertainty, expectations, unemployment, money, and policy stabilization as central. John Maynard Keynes anchors the family by arguing that economies can settle into underemployment and that state policy can matter. Joan Robinson, Nicholas Kaldor, Paul Samuelson, James Tobin, and later New Keynesians develop different branches of the tradition.

The pressure point is policy confidence. A good map should distinguish Keynesian insight about demand failure from overconfidence that governments can steer complex economies cleanly.

## Austrian economics

Austrian economics emphasizes subjective value, entrepreneurial discovery, price signals, dispersed knowledge, capital structure, and market process. Carl Menger starts the marginalist root. Ludwig von Mises and Friedrich Hayek develop calculation, knowledge, and spontaneous order arguments. Israel Kirzner emphasizes entrepreneurship and discovery.

The pressure point is institutions and power. The reviewer should ask whether market process accounts explain coordination well while underdescribing monopoly, inequality, public goods, or crisis.

## Institutional and evolutionary economics

Institutional economics studies economies as rule-bound, historically embedded systems shaped by habits, organizations, law, technology, and power. Thorstein Veblen, John R. Commons, and Wesley Mitchell anchor early institutionalism. Later institutional and evolutionary economists such as Douglass North, Geoffrey Hodgson, and Richard Nelson emphasize institutions, path dependence, routines, and technological change.

The pressure point is precision. This family can explain context and change well, but a reviewable map should ask whether it gives testable mechanisms or only rich description.

## Behavioral economics

Behavioral economics studies bounded rationality, heuristics, biases, framing, loss aversion, fairness, and real decision behavior. Herbert Simon is essential for bounded rationality. Daniel Kahneman, Amos Tversky, Richard Thaler, and Cass Sunstein help turn psychology into an economic research and policy program.

The pressure point is scope. The reviewer should ask whether behavioral findings revise economic theory deeply or become local patches inside a still-neoclassical frame.

## Complexity, ecological, and pluralist economics

Complexity and ecological economics treat economies as adaptive systems embedded in energy, ecology, networks, institutions, and feedback. W. Brian Arthur and Santa Fe style economics emphasize increasing returns, path dependence, and complex adaptive systems. Elinor Ostrom studies commons governance. Herman Daly and ecological economists put biophysical limits and sustainability at the center.

The pressure point is integration. A good map should ask whether these approaches form one school or several neighboring families linked by systems thinking, limits, and non-equilibrium dynamics.
